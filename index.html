The Basics

Why does protein matter so much?

Protein is one of three macronutrients (along with carbs and fat) — but it's the only one your body can't store for later. It's made of amino acids, the "building blocks" your body uses constantly to repair and grow.

💪

Builds & repairs muscle

Every workout creates tiny tears in muscle fibre. Protein supplies the amino acids used to repair and grow them back stronger.

🧬

Makes enzymes & hormones

Insulin, digestive enzymes, and many hormones are literally built from protein — your metabolism depends on it.

🛡️

Powers your immune system

Antibodies that fight infection are proteins. Low intake can quietly weaken your immunity.

🍽️

Keeps you full longer

Protein is the most satiating macronutrient — it slows digestion and curbs hunger hormones, helping control appetite.

🦴

Supports bones & skin

Collagen and keratin — key structural proteins — keep skin, hair, nails, and bones strong.

🔋

Backup energy source

When carbs run low, your body can break down protein for energy — though that's not its main job.

Beginner tip: A common starting target is about 0.8–1.2g of protein per kg of body weight for general health, and 1.6–2.2g/kg if you're actively trying to build muscle. These are general guidelines — individual needs vary.

The Science, Simplified

How protein fuels muscle growth

Muscle growth (technically "muscle protein synthesis") is a step-by-step process. Here's the simple version:

1

Training creates micro-damage

Resistance exercise (weights, bodyweight training) creates tiny, controlled tears in muscle fibres. This is a normal, necessary trigger for growth.

2

Protein breaks down into amino acids

Digestion breaks dietary protein into individual amino acids — especially leucine, which acts like a "switch" that turns on muscle repair.

3

Muscle protein synthesis (MPS) kicks in

Your body uses those amino acids to repair damaged fibres and add new proteins to the muscle — making it slightly bigger and stronger than before.

4

Repeat consistently → visible growth

One workout + one meal won't build noticeable muscle. It's the repeated cycle of training and adequate protein, over weeks and months, that compounds into real results.

The Science, Simplified

How protein supports fat loss

Protein doesn't "burn fat" directly — but it makes fat loss easier and more sustainable in three key ways:

1

Higher thermic effect

Your body burns more calories digesting protein (~20-30% of its calories) compared to carbs (~5-10%) or fat (~0-3%). More digestion effort = more calories used.

2

Keeps hunger in check

Protein boosts satiety hormones and slows stomach emptying, so you naturally feel fuller — making it easier to eat fewer calories without constant hunger.

3

Protects muscle during a calorie deficit

When eating less, your body can break down muscle for energy. Enough protein signals your body to preserve muscle and lose fat instead.

4

Result: a leaner, more defined look

Losing fat while keeping muscle is what creates visible "toning" — not just a lower number on the scale.

Beginner tip: Fat loss ultimately depends on a calorie deficit — protein simply makes that deficit easier to sustain and protects your hard-earned muscle while you're in it.

Quick Add-on

Bonus: Fibre — protein's quiet partner

Fibre doesn't build muscle, but it keeps digestion, blood sugar, and heart health in check — and most high-protein vegetarian foods (legumes, whole grains) already contain it.

Soluble fibre

Dissolves in water, forms a gel. Slows digestion, helps control blood sugar and cholesterol. Found in oats, beans, apples.

Insoluble fibre

Doesn't dissolve — adds bulk and speeds food through the gut. Supports regularity. Found in whole wheat, vegetable skins, nuts.

General target: 25–30g of fibre per day for most adults.

  • Chia seeds (100g) ~34g
  • Lentils, cooked (100g) ~8g
  • Rajma / kidney beans, cooked (100g) ~6.4g
  • Chickpeas, cooked (100g) ~7.6g
  • Oats, dry (100g) ~10g
  • Almonds (100g) ~12.5g
  • Whole wheat / atta (100g) ~11g
  • Pear with skin (100g) ~3.1g
  • Broccoli, cooked (100g) ~3.3g
